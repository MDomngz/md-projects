Chapter One · The Centerpiece

Susanna Wright, 1697–1784

An 18th-century poet, botanist, business owner, and legal scholar, influential in the political economy of colonial Pennsylvania.

Born in 1697 in Warrington, Lancashire, to Quaker parents, Susanna remained in England to finish her schooling and crossed the Atlantic years after her family, arriving already fluent in French and versed in Latin and Italian — deeply familiar with the poetry, philosophy, and scientific curiosity of her age.

From the stone house at Wright's Ferry she ran a working frontier estate, ministered to the Shawnee, practiced informal law for her neighbors, and raised silkworms for a local silk industry. She lived there until her death in 1784, at 87.

Silk to a queen

Susanna's silkworms were no idle hobby. Folklore — repeated for generations — holds that cloth from her Susquehanna silk reached Queen Charlotte by way of Benjamin Franklin; at a London birthday celebration for King George III, the queen is said to have worn a gown made from sixty yards of Susquehanna Valley silk. A symbolic journey from a frontier riverbank to the British court.

Letters with Franklin

She bought books through London booksellers, including Franklin, and the two became correspondents and friends. Franklin sent her presents alongside her purchases — a thermometer, an almanac, bayberry candles; in return the Franklins received casks of pickled Susquehanna salmon, baskets of apples, and her letters. He visited Wright's Ferry Mansion while experimenting with electrical current.

Her surviving poetry — circulated quietly among friends rather than printed — reveals a sustained examination of justice, reason, and moral authority. On the verse of Susanna Wright

A proto-feminist mind

She wrote not for reputation or posterity but as a form of inquiry — using verse to test ideas, challenge assumptions, and speak plainly under the cover of reflection. In an age that gave women little public voice, she engaged difficult subjects head-on. Her desk is one of the site's signature rooms.

Chapter Three · Freedom

A beacon of liberty on the river

For freedom seekers moving north, the Susquehanna was both a barrier and a threshold — and Columbia, with its large free Black community, was a known place to reach the far bank.

The river that made the town a gateway for commerce made it a gateway for liberty too. On the eastern shore lay a community of free Black residents — entrepreneurs, laborers, and families — alongside Quaker neighbors willing to risk a great deal. Together they formed a working station on the Underground Railroad, one of the better-documented crossings on the lower Susquehanna.

The Mifflin House

The Mifflins, a Quaker family on the western approach to the river, operated their house as a stop on the Underground Railroad. The home has since received national recognition as an Underground Railroad site — a rare surviving anchor of the network. The family worked closely with a boatman who knew the water in the dark.

Robert Loney's night crossings

Robert Loney — born enslaved in Virginia in 1815, freed and settled in Columbia after 1819 — used a rowboat or raft to carry freedom seekers across the Susquehanna in the cover of night. Columbia's large free Black population gave those who reached the eastern bank a place to disappear into safety and continue north.

Wealth turned toward freedom

Columbia's free Black community was not only numerous — it was, in places, prosperous. Stephen Smith and William Whipper built one of the state's premier lumber, coal, and real-estate enterprises, demonstrating the plain benefits of northern freedom. That same prosperity funded resistance: support for the railroad, for moral-reform societies, and for the legal defense of those the law sought to return south.

The river carried more than cargo — it carried people toward freedom. A Rivertown Story

The toll of the Fugitive Slave Act

The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 turned every northern town into contested ground. Slave-catchers could pursue freedom seekers — and free Black residents wrongly claimed as property — deep into Pennsylvania, with the force of federal law behind them. For a community like Columbia's, perched at a crossing, the danger was constant and personal. Liberty here was never simply geographic; it had to be defended, again and again, in the open.

A note on honesty

Columbia's role as a refuge sits beside the violence of the 1834 riots, when this same town turned on its Black residents. We hold both truths at once. The town was a beacon — and a place where that light was attacked.

Chapter Four · An Unflinching Room

The riots of 1834

Before you read

This chapter describes racist violence against Black residents of Columbia. We tell it plainly and with respect for the people who endured it. Period sources used derisive language for Black neighborhoods and people; where such terms appear in the record we contextualize them — we do not adopt their spirit.

In a town that helped people escape slavery, white mobs turned on their Black neighbors — twice in a single year — to drive prosperous Black families out and seize their property cheap.

The contradiction is the point. Columbia was a station on the Underground Railroad and home to one of Pennsylvania's wealthiest Black entrepreneurs. It was also, in 1834, a town where economic resentment and racism boiled over into organized violence. An abolitionist stronghold is not the same as a town free of hatred — and Columbia's story holds both at once.

The target: Stephen Smith

Stephen Smith — born enslaved, self-emancipated, and by the 1830s one of the most successful lumber and real-estate men in the state — was exactly the kind of Black prosperity that enraged a faction of the white town. His success was read as an affront and an obstacle.

August 1834

A white mob attacked Smith's office, spurring a race riot. The aim was explicit: to frighten Smith and other Black property owners into selling below market value and leaving town. Rioters even charged that Smith had inflated the value of his own property — a pretext for the violence.

October 1834

A second riot followed. The attacks targeted Black-owned property — including attempts on Smith's lumber yard — and the homes and livelihoods of the broader Black community along the waterfront district.

Why it happened

The Columbia riots did not happen in isolation. 1834 saw anti-Black and anti-abolitionist violence flare across the North — in Philadelphia, in New York, in towns across New Jersey and Pennsylvania. The pattern was consistent: white working-class economic anxiety, fanned by racism and the rhetoric of pro-slavery interests, aimed squarely at the Black communities making the most visible economic gains.

In Columbia the local accelerant was clear. A free Black community was not merely surviving but thriving — owning land, running businesses, accumulating wealth. For those who believed that prosperity belonged to white men alone, Smith and his neighbors were a standing rebuke. The riots were an attempt to erase that rebuke by force.

Truth is the brand. The triumphs of this town and its worst day are told with the same care — because both are true, and one does not cancel the other. Editorial principle · A Rivertown Story

What it means

Columbia's Black community did not vanish. Smith and Whipper continued to build, to fund the Underground Railroad, and to resist. But the 1834 riots are a permanent part of the record: proof that the "frontier of freedom" was contested ground, defended by the very people it most endangered. A town marking its 300th year owes its whole record the same care — to tell the bridge fire and the ferry without telling this would be to package the town rather than honor it.

A note on names — Columbia, PA, not Tennessee

These are the events of Columbia, Pennsylvania, in 1834. They are not the 20th-century events in Columbia, Tennessee — the 1946 "Mink Slide" riot in Maury County, Tennessee, is a separate history. The term "Mink Slide" belongs to that Tennessee story, not to this one. We keep the two distinct on purpose.

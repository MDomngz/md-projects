The Story

Three Centuries,
One Chronicle

1726 · 2026

The full sweep of the town, century by century, in a single reading

I · The Crossing Wright's Ferry II · Fire & Freedom The Burning Bridge III · Keeping the Past

Follow the chronology like the river itself — from a single log cabin near Shawnee Creek to a town celebrating its three hundredth year.

A tricentennial divides neatly into three. Columbia's first century was the century of the crossing — ferry, wagons, and a name with national ambition. Its second was fire and freedom — the Underground Railroad, the riots, the world's longest covered bridge and the night it burned. Its third has been the long, patient work of keeping the past. All three are here, in order.

The First Century

The Crossing

1726 – 1826
1724

A Quaker comes to the river

John Wright, an English Quaker from Chester County, travels to the eastern bank of the Susquehanna to explore the land and preach to the Shawnee living along Shawnee Creek. He builds a log cabin and stays more than a year on a tract first granted by William Penn.

1726

The settlement takes root — year zero

Wright returns with Robert Barber and Samuel Blunston. They begin developing the area, with Wright building a house about a hundred yards from the river near present-day South Second and Union Streets. The place is first known simply as Wright's Ferry. Three hundred years later, this is the anniversary the town now marks.

1730

The ferry is licensed

John Wright is granted a patent to operate a ferry across the Susquehanna. With Barber and Blunston, he establishes Wright's Ferry — and a crossing that will grow towns on both banks: Columbia to the east, Wrightsville to the west.

1738

Susanna Wright's house of stone

A substantial stone dwelling rises at Second and Cherry — later known as Wright's Ferry Mansion. It becomes the home of Susanna Wright: poet, botanist, legal scholar, and silk-maker, a rare point of permanence in a landscape still defined by movement. Her story →

1788

A name with national ambition

The town is laid out and renamed Columbia — a patriotic flourish in the young republic. It is even briefly considered as a site for the national capital.

1814

The first bridge across

The first covered bridge connects Columbia and Wrightsville, replacing the ferry's slow crossings. Conestoga wagons that once waited days to cross now roll over the water.

In Depth · The Gateway West

Wright's Ferry: before the bridges, there was the boat

Wright's Ferry turned a remote stretch of wilderness into the site of permanent civilization — the place where the road west met the river.

In 1730 John Wright secured a patent to run a ferry across the Susquehanna, and with Robert Barber and Samuel Blunston he established the crossing that bore his name. It was more than a convenience. The ferry made the eastern bank a destination and the western bank a frontier — and growth followed the landings, raising Columbia on one shore and Wrightsville on the other.

The wait

This was the great artery west, and traffic outran the boats. Conestoga wagons — the freight haulers of the colonial frontier — could wait days for their turn across the water, their wagons sometimes chalk-numbered to keep order in the line. The riverbank became a rough town of its own: teamsters, travelers, livestock, and cargo, all paused at the edge of the continent's next chapter.

The fares

Crossing the Susquehanna was a transaction, set by license and custom. A coach-and-horses crossing might run on the order of nine shillings — no small sum — while foot passengers, riders, and droves of animals each carried their own rate. The ferry was infrastructure and enterprise at once, a toll on the movement of an expanding country.

Wright's Ferry was the "gateway to the west" — a place that turned a remote wilderness into the site of permanent civilization. Foundations of a Rivertown

The ferry could only carry so much. As traffic grew, the crossing evolved: a first covered bridge linked Columbia and Wrightsville in 1814, replacing slow boat trips with a continuous span. Then, in the town's second century, came its successor — a marvel of engineering with a violent destiny.

The Second Century

Fire & Freedom

1826 – 1926
1830s

A free Black community and a refuge

Columbia is home to a large free Black population and entrepreneurs like Stephen Smith and William Whipper. With nearby Quaker families, the town becomes a known station on the Underground Railroad. Freedom & the railroad →

1834

The riots

In an abolitionist stronghold, white mobs attack the Black community and the property of Stephen Smith in August and again in October — an attempt to drive prosperous Black landowners from town. The hardest chapter, told honestly. What happened →

1834

The longest covered bridge in the world

A new bridge opens between Columbia and Wrightsville on July 8 — over a mile long at 5,620 feet, resting on 27 piers. Built by James Moore and John Evans for $157,300, it is the longest covered bridge on earth.

1863

The bridge burns

June 28. As Confederate forces advance during the Gettysburg Campaign, Union troops set the great covered bridge alight — saturated with crude oil from a Columbia refinery — to deny the enemy the crossing. It burns for six hours. The army turns back. The full night, below ↓

Late 1800s

Iron, rail, and river commerce

Columbia thrives as a railroad and industrial hub on the Susquehanna — furnaces, lumber, and the constant traffic of a working river town.

In Depth · June 28, 1863

The choice that turned an army

By the summer of 1863, the Confederate Army was deep in Pennsylvania. Between it and the state capital stood a river — and a bridge over a mile long.

1834
The bridge's opening year

The stakes

There were only three bridges across the lower Susquehanna during the war: the Camelback at Harrisburg, the Conowingo in Maryland, and this one. If Confederate forces could cross here, they could enter Columbia, seize horses and supplies, cut rail lines, threaten the capital at Harrisburg — and perhaps strike toward Philadelphia. The bridge was a door into the heart of the North, and it was standing wide open.

As the army advanced

A line drawn at the water

Confederate forces pressed toward Wrightsville on the western bank. Union militia and townspeople, unable to hold the crossing by arms, prepared to deny it entirely. Earlier, they had saturated the bridge with crude oil drawn from a Columbia refinery — turning the great wooden structure into a fuse.

The decision

To destroy their own crossing

It was a wrenching choice. The bridge was the town's link to the wider world, an engineering marvel and a point of pride. But standing, it was an invitation. As the Confederates reached the bridge, Union forces set it alight near the Wrightsville side.

The fire

Six hours of flame over the river

Soaked in oil, the structure caught fast and burned completely in roughly six hours — a mile of fire reflected on the Susquehanna, visible for miles. The longest covered bridge in the world was gone by morning.

The consequence

An army turned back

With the crossing destroyed, the Confederate advance on this front was checked. Lancaster County was spared, and the campaign's momentum shifted — helping set the stage for the three days at Gettysburg that began on July 1. A small river town's hardest decision rippled into the war's turning point.

What remains

The stone piers that once carried the bridge still rise from the Susquehanna, visible from the Columbia riverfront. They are among the most affecting things to see in town: the bones of a record-setting bridge, and of the night a community burned its own crossing to stop a war from reaching home. The Third Century

Keeping the Past

1926 – 2026
20th c.

Preservation becomes the work

As industry ebbs, Columbia turns to what it has kept: the 1738 stone mansion restored and opened for tours, the historic streetscapes maintained, the record of the town's hardest and finest hours held onto rather than paved over.

Today

The antique capital

More than a dozen antique markets fill the town's restored historic buildings — Columbia recognized as an antique capital. The instinct for craft and trade that started at the ferry landing never left; it moved indoors. Visit today →

2026

Three hundred years

The tricentennial. Wright's Ferry Mansion stands where Susanna Wright kept her silk and her letters; the bridge piers stand in the river; the riverfront and rail-trail carry walkers where wagons once waited days to cross. The town marks its 300th year the way it has kept all the others — by telling the story whole.

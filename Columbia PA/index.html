From Wright's Ferry to the frontier of freedom — a town, and its story, turn 300

The Story · 1726 → 2026 People & Freedom Visit & Archive

In 1726 a Quaker ferryman put down roots on the eastern bank of the Susquehanna. Three hundred years later, the town he started is still here — and so is its story.

Columbia's three centuries run from a ferry landing that became the gateway west, through a polymath woman who corresponded with Franklin, across a covered bridge burned to stop an army, and into a present defined by preservation and craft. This site is the town's tricentennial telling of that story — not a brochure and not a museum placard, but a place for a great story about a great community, told with the texture of the river it sits beside.

The Moments That Stay With You

Three hundred years, six moments

The Burning Bridge

On June 28, 1863, the longest covered bridge in the world was set ablaze to stop the Confederate advance — and helped set the stage for Gettysburg.

A Beacon of Liberty

A large free Black community and a network of safe houses made Columbia a known destination on the Underground Railroad.

Susanna Wright

Poet, botanist, legal scholar, and silk-maker who traded letters — and salmon — with Benjamin Franklin from the frontier.

The 1834 Riots

An abolitionist stronghold convulsed by anti-Black violence aimed at men like Stephen Smith. We tell it without flinching.

The Gateway West

Wright's Ferry turned a remote wilderness into a crossing — Conestoga wagons waiting days for their turn over the water.

Antique Capital

Today, over a dozen markets in restored historic buildings carry the town's old trade in craft into the present.

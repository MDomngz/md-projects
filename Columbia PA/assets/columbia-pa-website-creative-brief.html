Website Creative Brief

Columbia, Pennsylvania
From Wright's Ferry to the Frontier of Freedom

✶
Prepared for the Columbia Heritage Web Project · A Rivertown Story
Section 01

The Big Idea

A digital home for one small Susquehanna town that carried an outsized chapter of American history.

This site is not a brochure and not a museum placard. It is a place for a great story about a great community — told with the texture of the river it sits beside. Columbia's narrative runs from a Quaker ferryman's vision in 1724, through a polymath woman who corresponded with Franklin, across a covered bridge burned to stop an army, and into a present defined by preservation and craft. The website should make a visitor feel they've stepped onto the eastern bank of the Susquehanna and been handed the keys to the whole story.

Everything that follows — voice, palette, typography, structure — exists to serve one feeling: authentic depth. The design should read like a well-kept town archive that happens to be beautiful, not a tourism template with history poured in.

Wright's Ferry was the "gateway to the west" — a place that turned a remote wilderness into the site of permanent civilization. Source theme — Foundations of a Rivertown
Section 02

Goals & Success

Show breadth

Present the full sweep — history, notable residents, the Underground Railroad, the 1834 race riots, the bridge fire, and the modern antique trade — without flattening any of it.

Feel authentic

The design itself should belong to the content: aged, riverine, hand-kept. No generic "history-site" tropes.

Hold complexity honestly

Tell the hard parts (the riots, "Mink Slide," the Fugitive Slave Act) with the same care as the triumphs. Truth is the brand.

Invite the visit

Move a curious reader toward Wright's Ferry Mansion, the antique markets, and the riverfront — turning a click into a trip.

What success looks like

  • A first-time visitor can name three things Columbia is known for after five minutes.
  • A researcher trusts the site enough to cite it or dig deeper.
  • A resident feels the town's identity is honored, not packaged.
  • Visitors leave with at least one concrete reason to come in person.
Section 03

Audience

A flagship site serving three readers at once. The design must let each find their level without making the others feel lost.

Heritage Tourists & Visitors History Buffs & Researchers Residents & Local Pride

The Visitor

Planning a day trip. Wants the highlights, the must-sees, and the practical "how do I get there."

The Researcher

Hungry for depth, dates, names, primary detail. Rewards credibility and rigor.

The Resident

Looking for belonging. Wants the town's story told with pride and accuracy.

Design principle: layered reading. Bold, scannable highlights on top; deeper archival detail one click or one scroll beneath. Never force the casual reader through the scholarship, never starve the scholar of it.

Section 04

Voice & Tone

Part journalist, part researcher, part historian — a knowledgeable local who can tell a great story straight.

Write with the confidence of someone who has walked the riverbank a thousand times and read every deed in the county. Vivid but never purple. Reverent of the past but unafraid of its contradictions. The "burning bridge" and the "frontier of freedom" earn their drama because the facts are true.

Do

  • Lead with the human moment, then ground it in fact.
  • Use period detail (chalk-numbered wagons, silkworms, 9 shillings a coach).
  • Name names — Susanna Wright, Robert Loney, William Wright.
  • Let hard history sit honestly beside the triumphs.

Don't

  • Slip into brochure-speak or chamber-of-commerce gloss.
  • Sanitize the 1834 riots or the racial climate of "The Bottom."
  • Overload casual readers with citations on the surface.
  • Treat the town as a backdrop instead of the protagonist.
Section 05

Content Architecture

A spine of chronology, with thematic "deep dive" rooms hanging off it so every audience can wander their own way.

  • Home — "A Rivertown Story"
    Cinematic opening on the Susquehanna. The big idea, three doorways (Story · People · Visit), and a teaser of the most arresting moments.
  • The Story (Timeline)
    1724 → today as a scrollable river of dates: founding, the ferry, the bridges, the Civil War fire, modern preservation.
  • People of Columbia
    Susanna Wright as the centerpiece; profiles of William Wright, Robert Loney, the five who burned the bridge.
  • Freedom & the Underground Railroad
    The "beacon of liberty" — safe houses, the Mifflin House, "The Bottom," the Fugitive Slave Act's toll.
  • The 1834 Race Riots
    A standalone, unflinching room. Labor resentment, the firebombing of "The Bottom," and what it means in an abolitionist stronghold.
  • The Burning Bridge (1863)
    June 28, 1863 — the choice that turned an army. A single-moment narrative page.
  • Wright's Ferry & the Gateway West
    Ferry life, fares, the covered bridges, Conestoga wagons waiting days to cross.
  • Visit Today — Antique Capital
    Wright's Ferry Mansion plus the antique destinations; the bridge from 18th-century craft to today's economy.
  • Archive / Sources
    Where researchers go to verify. Signals credibility to the whole site.
Section 06

Visual Direction

A river archive: aged paper, slate water, ferry brass, and a single ember of warning.

The mood sits at the meeting of colonial print culture and the Susquehanna itself. Think letterpress broadsides, surveyor's maps, and silk ledgers — but breathing, with the cool depth of river water underneath. Restraint is the rule; the history supplies the drama.

Palette

Old Ink#1C1814
Aged Paper#ECE0C8
Susquehanna Slate#33576B
Bridge Ember#9C3B21
Ferry Brass#B48A3E
Quaker Moss#5C6B48

Paper and ink carry the page. Slate-blue anchors the river and primary navigation. Brass is the accent of craft and ferry-era commerce. Ember is rationed — used only for the moments of consequence (the riots, the bridge fire, key calls-to-action), so its appearance always means something.

Typography

Wright's Ferry

Display / Headlines — a high-contrast old-style serif (e.g. Caslon, Iowan Old Style). Letterpress lineage, set large with generous swash italics for titles.

The river carried more than cargo — it carried people toward freedom.

Body — a readable transitional serif at 17–19px, long measure, warm leading. Built for paragraphs of real history.

Section · Dateline · Label

Accent — a quiet grotesque/gothic sans (e.g. Trade Gothic), all caps with wide tracking, for kickers, datelines, and navigation only.

Imagery & Texture

  • Photography: the living river, stone of Wright's Ferry Mansion, weathered antique-shop interiors, the bridge's surviving piers — shot warm, with shadow and patina.
  • Archival: period engravings, surveyor's maps, Susanna Wright's poetry and silk, the covered bridge in old prints — treated as treasured artifacts, framed and captioned with care.
  • Texture: subtle paper grain, double-rule dividers, and map-line ornaments. Texture should whisper, never shout.
  • Motion: slow and water-like — gentle parallax on the river, timeline that flows on scroll. Nothing flashy; the past does not rush.
Section 07

Signature Moments

Three set-pieces that make the site memorable and prove the design can carry weight.

The Burning Bridge

A scroll-driven retelling of June 28, 1863 — the ember palette earns its keep here as the longest covered bridge in the world goes up to stop the Confederate advance.

Susanna's Desk

An interactive room for the polymath — her silk to Queen Charlotte, her proto-feminist verse, her letters to Franklin — staged like an open archive drawer.

The River at Night

Robert Loney ferrying freedom seekers across in darkness — a quiet, map-led narrative that honors the Underground Railroad without spectacle.

Section 08

Guardrails

  • Distinguish Columbia, Pennsylvania from Columbia, Tennessee — the 1834 riots are not the 20th-century Tennessee events.
  • Handle "Mink Slide," "The Bottom," and the riots with historical honesty and human dignity; contextualize derisive period language, never adopt its spirit.
  • Keep facts verifiable; the Archive/Sources page is load-bearing for trust.
  • Accessibility is non-negotiable: strong contrast, real alt text on every archival image, keyboard-navigable timeline.
Section 09

Recommended Next Steps

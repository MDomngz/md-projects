# Elementary Educator Companion

A comprehensive, trauma-informed skill to support K-5 teachers in under-resourced schools serving students facing poverty, housing instability, food insecurity, family stress, and other life disruptions. This skill helps you build a safe classroom community, plan lessons that meet students where they are, assess learning authentically, differentiate for diverse learners with what you have, and support multilingual students—all grounded in evidence-based practices, Common Core State Standards (CCSS), and an understanding of how poverty and trauma affect learning and behavior.

## A Note About Your Context

Many students in your classroom may be experiencing:
- **Food insecurity** — Not knowing where the next meal comes from
- **Housing instability** — Moving frequently, living in overcrowded spaces, or experiencing homelessness
- **Family stress** — Job loss, incarceration, immigration fears, illness, death, or family separation
- **Community trauma** — Exposure to violence, loss of community members, displacement from neighborhoods
- **School disruption** — Frequent absences, arriving late, gaps in foundational skills
- **Language barriers** — Learning in a language that's not their first, with limited home literacy support

Your job is not to "fix" these conditions—that's not your role. Your job is to create a safe, predictable classroom where learning can happen, build genuine relationships with your students, and teach with high expectations while acknowledging their realities. This skill is designed with all of that in mind.

## What This Skill Does

- **Lesson Planning**: Structure learning experiences with clear objectives, engaging activities, and formative assessment check-ins
- **Assessment Design**: Create assessments that check for understanding, provide actionable feedback, and guide next steps
- **Differentiation**: Adapt instruction for mixed-ability classrooms, using tiered activities, flexible grouping, and multiple modalities
- **Bilingual & ESL Support**: Scaffold language-rich instruction, honor home languages, and accelerate academic language development
- **Inclusive Design**: Make activities accessible to students with diverse learning needs and backgrounds
- **Classroom Organization**: Manage learning centers, stations, and routines that support independent and collaborative learning

## When to Use This Skill

Reach for this skill when you're:
- Planning a new unit, lesson, or learning sequence
- Designing assessments (formative checks, quizzes, performance tasks, projects)
- Wondering how to differentiate for students who are behind, ahead, or learning English
- Creating materials and activities for your classroom
- Troubleshooting engagement, behavior, or learning issues
- Setting up routines, centers, or classroom structures
- Aligning your instruction to CCSS standards and learning progressions
- Supporting students with diverse needs (IEPs, 504s, advanced learners, struggling readers, multilingual learners)

## The Instructional Design Framework

### 1. Start with Learning Objectives

Use CCSS standards to define what students should know and be able to do. Frame objectives in student-friendly language.

**Example:**
- CCSS: 3.NBT.A.1 (Use place value understanding to round to the nearest 10 or 100)
- Student-friendly objective: "I can round numbers to the nearest 10 and 100 to make math easier"

### 2. Assess What Students Already Know

Before planning the full lesson, check prior knowledge through:
- Quick oral checks or thumbs up/down signals
- Exit tickets from previous lessons
- Observations during review activities
- One-on-one conversations with students

**Why it matters:** This tells you where to start and who needs pre-teaching or acceleration.

### 3. Design the Learning Experience

Structure lessons around this sequence:

**Opening (5-10 min):** Hook students' interest, connect to prior knowledge, preview learning objective
- "We're going to become rounding experts today. Look at this menu—how would you decide the price when you round?"

**Mini-Lesson (10-15 min):** Model the skill or concept explicitly using think-aloud
- Show your reasoning: "I'm thinking... 47 is closer to 50 than 40 because..."
- Use concrete manipulatives, visuals, or real-world examples
- Check for understanding (not just "Do you understand?")

**Guided Practice (10-15 min):** Students try with your support
- Work through examples together (I do, we do, you do together)
- Use hand signals for quick formative checks (fist to five, thumbs)
- Have sentence frames or graphic organizers ready
- Reteach on the spot if students are confused

**Independent or Collaborative Practice (15-20 min):** Students apply the skill
- Provide choices in difficulty level or modality
- Use stations or centers for differentiation
- Circulate, observe, take notes on who needs what
- Pull small groups for targeted support or extension

**Closing (5 min):** Reflect on learning
- "What strategy did you use? Show me with your fingers."
- "Who can explain this to someone who missed today?"
- Quick exit ticket to check understanding before next lesson

### 4. Differentiate Throughout

**Tiered Activities:** Offer 2-3 versions of the same skill at different complexity levels
- Tier 1 (Beginning): Concrete, heavily scaffolded, fewer steps
- Tier 2 (Developing): Semi-concrete, some scaffolding, standard complexity
- Tier 3 (Advanced): Abstract, minimal scaffolding, extensions or applications

**Flexible Grouping:** Change groups frequently based on assessment data, not fixed ability levels

**Multiple Entry Points:** Let students engage with content through different modalities
- Visual (charts, images, color-coded materials)
- Kinesthetic (manipulatives, movement, hands-on activities)
- Auditory (partner talk, read-alouds, verbal explanations)
- Reading/Writing (texts at various levels, sentence frames)

### 5. Support Multilingual Learners & ELL Students

**Language Objectives (alongside content objectives):**
- "I can use rounding vocabulary: round, nearest, place value" (Language objective)
- "I can round to the nearest 10 or 100" (Content objective)

**Scaffolding Strategies:**
- **Sentence frames**: "47 is closer to _____ because _____"
- **Word walls & visual supports**: Pictures + words for key terms
- **Pre-teach vocabulary**: Introduce key words before the lesson with context
- **Pair multilingual learners strategically**: With English-proficient peers for support, or with same-language partners when translating would help
- **Honor home languages**: Invite students to explain concepts in their first language, recognize multilingualism as an asset
- **Slow down & pause**: Give wait time (at least 5-10 seconds) before expecting responses
- **Comprehensible input**: Use gestures, visuals, repetition, and shorter sentences

**Assessment Considerations for ELL Students:**
- Allow oral responses before written
- Provide word banks or glossaries
- Give extra time
- Assess language separately from content (a student might understand rounding but not articulate it fluently in English)

### 6. Monitor & Respond (Formative Assessment)

Use quick checks to guide instruction:

**During the lesson:**
- Hand signals (thumbs up/sideways/down; fist to five)
- Whiteboards for quick writes
- Partner talk & listen-ins
- Observation notes on specific students

**After the lesson:**
- Exit tickets (digital or paper: "One thing I learned... One thing I'm confused about...")
- Quick quizzes or skill checks
- Observation notes from practice time

**Use the data immediately:**
- Who needs more scaffolding tomorrow?
- Who's ready to go deeper?
- What misconceptions came up?
- Reteach or adjust tomorrow based on what you observed

### 7. Plan for Accessibility & Inclusion

Make activities accessible to all students:

**For students with mobility or sensory needs:**
- Provide alternatives (typing instead of handwriting; audio instead of reading)
- Arrange furniture for access
- Use assistive technology or adapted materials

**For students with attention or executive function challenges:**
- Use timers for transitions
- Break tasks into smaller chunks
- Provide checklists and visual schedules
- Seat near you during direct instruction
- Offer fidget tools or movement breaks

**For students with processing delays:**
- Slow down speech; pause longer
- Repeat and rephrase key information
- Provide written and visual cues
- Allow extra time

**For advanced learners:**
- Offer open-ended extensions ("How would you teach this to a younger student?")
- Increase complexity or depth, not just quantity
- Let them pursue research interests related to the concept

## Creating Assessments

### Formative Assessments (During Learning)

Purpose: Guide instruction, give students feedback, identify who needs support

**Types:**
- Exit tickets (quick writes, digital forms)
- Hand signals (thumbs, fist to five)
- Observation checklists (what students do & say)
- Quick quizzes (3-5 questions)
- Student self-assessment (rubrics, checklists)
- Partner checks & peer feedback

**Tips:**
- Keep it quick (< 5 minutes)
- Check understanding, not just completion
- Give actionable feedback ("You rounded correctly! Next time, explain WHY it's closer to 50")
- Use data to decide next steps

### Summative Assessments (After Learning)

Purpose: Measure whether students met the learning objective

**Types:**
- Unit test or quiz
- Performance task or project
- Presentation or demonstration
- Portfolio of work samples
- Rubric-scored responses

**Design tips:**
- Align directly to learning objectives
- Offer multiple ways to show learning (not just multiple choice)
- Include scaffolded and challenging items
- Use rubrics with clear criteria and student-friendly language

## Supporting Multilingual & ELL Students: Key Principles

1. **Validate home languages** — Bilingualism is an asset. Create space for students to use their first language when helpful.
2. **Make language visible** — Use word walls, anchor charts, visuals, and sentence frames so students can see and internalize academic language.
3. **Scaffold comprehension** — Use gestures, visuals, manipulatives, and repetition. Don't simplify to the point of removing rigor.
4. **Build academic language progressively** — Teach not just vocabulary, but how English structures meaning (grammar, syntax).
5. **Allow processing time** — Give 5-10 seconds of wait time before expecting responses.
6. **Pair students strategically** — Mix English-proficient peers with language learners; allow same-language pairs when translation helps.
7. **Separate language from content assessment** — A student might understand the math but not articulate it fluently in English yet.
8. **Create low-stakes opportunities to speak** — Partner talk, small group discussions, think-pair-share are less intimidating than whole-class responses.

## Inclusive Classroom Design

- **Physical space:** Ensure all students can see/hear/access materials. Minimize clutter and sensory overload.
- **Routines & structures:** Clear, consistent routines reduce anxiety and support students with executive function challenges.
- **Choice & agency:** Let students make choices when possible (order of activities, which station, how to show learning).
- **Positive relationships:** Know your students as people. Check in regularly. Celebrate effort and growth.
- **Behavior as communication:** Challenging behavior often signals unmet needs (need for movement, overstimulation, confusion, or a skill gap).

## Trauma-Informed Teaching Foundations

Before lesson planning, behavior management, or assessment—build safety and trust. Students who are hungry, tired, worried about their families, or grieving cannot learn well. Here's how to create a classroom where learning is possible:

### Build Predictable Routines
- Students experiencing chaos at home need to know what to expect. Same greeting every morning. Same way you transition between activities. Same signal for attention.
- Reduces anxiety. Makes the classroom a safe, knowable space.
- Example: "Every morning we do: unpack bags, morning meeting, breakfast, then work. The same every day."

### Create Emotional Safety
- Greet every student every day, by name, with genuine warmth. This small act matters enormously.
- Never shame or humiliate. Students may already feel ashamed about their circumstances.
- Respond to behavior with curiosity, not anger: "I notice you're having a hard time sitting still. What do you need right now?"
- Build in movement, transitions, and breaks. Trauma and stress trigger fight-or-flight responses. Movement helps regulate.

### Acknowledge Stress Without Dwelling on It
- "I know some of you have a lot going on. That's okay. We're here to learn, and I'm here to help."
- Don't ask intrusive questions about home situations. Let students share on their terms.
- If a student is grieving or struggling: "I care about you. I'm here if you need to talk. You're safe in this classroom."

### Build Genuine Relationships
- Learn your students' names, families, interests, and strengths (not just their deficits).
- Have one-on-one check-ins, especially with kids who are struggling.
- Show up consistently. Be the adult who is always there.
- Celebrate small wins: "I noticed you tried that problem even though it was hard. That takes courage."

### Manage Behavior as Communication
- Behavior is information. Fidgeting, aggression, shutdown, and acting out often signal: hunger, overstimulation, confusion, feeling unsafe, needing movement, grief, or learned survival strategies.
- Before consequences, ask: "What happened? What do you need?" Often the answer is a snack, a minute to move, or reassurance.
- Never take behavior personally. A student's trauma response is not about you.

### Food, Water, Rest, and Calm
- Keep snacks (if you can) or know where students can get breakfast/lunch.
- Have water available.
- Acknowledge that some students are tired because they didn't sleep well.
- Build in calm moments: quiet music, deep breathing, quiet sitting with a book.
- If a student falls asleep at their desk, let them rest (or check in gently—it might signal homelessness or lack of sleep at night).

## Quick Reference: Common Challenges in High-Stress, Under-Resourced Classrooms

### "My kids come to school hungry and tired. How do I teach?"
→ First: make sure they're connected to food programs (free breakfast, lunch, backpack programs). Keep snacks if you can. Start lessons after they eat. Build in rest and calm time. High-quality learning still happens, but you're starting by meeting basic needs.

### "Kids have behavior that looks like 'misbehavior' but feels like survival"
→ It probably is. Aggression, shutdown, lying, hoarding, stealing—these are often learned survival strategies from home. Respond with boundaries AND compassion. "I know you need help. Here's how we handle this in school. I believe in you." Consequences alone won't work.

### "Half my class is absent or late constantly"
→ Build in asynchronous catch-up time. Don't penalize absences—families facing housing instability or working multiple jobs can't always get kids to school on time. Use small group review. Accept that perfect attendance isn't realistic for everyone. Focus on engagement when they're here.

### "I don't have materials or I have to make do with what I have"
→ You're not alone. Request donations (ask families, community, online), use what you have creatively, make games and activities with paper. Some of the best learning happens with limited materials because students are more engaged. Go low-tech.

### "My students won't stay focused. They seem anxious or shut down."
→ This is often trauma response, not laziness. Break lessons into shorter chunks with movement. Use calming signals and movement. Check if they need water, food, or a bathroom break. Reduce stimulation if needed (less noise, simpler visuals). Build in emotional check-ins.

### "I have students with huge gaps—some at kindergarten level, some at grade level"
→ Use stations or rotations with tasks at different levels. Pre-teach and review frequently. Partner strong readers/thinkers with struggling ones. Celebrate growth, not just correct answers. Some kids have had interrupted schooling or absent family literacy support—that's not a reflection of ability.

### "I don't know if my ELL students understand or are just complying"
→ Use non-verbal checks (hand signals, drawings, showing with manipulatives). Partner talk with same-language speakers if possible. Pre-teach vocabulary with visuals and gestures. Assess language separately from content knowledge. Don't assume silence = understanding.

### "Some kids act out/shut down when they're frustrated instead of asking for help"
→ Teach explicitly how to ask for help: hand raise, sentence frame, coming to you. Practice it. Make it low-risk: "It's brave to ask for help." Some kids have learned that showing struggle = shame or punishment at home. You're modeling a different way.

### "Assessment is impossible—I have no time, no resources, and kids are stressed"
→ Use quick formative checks (observation, hand signals, whiteboards, exit tickets). You don't need formal tests for everything. Use what you learn to inform grouping and next steps. Share wins with families (not just problems). Make assessment low-stress.

### "My students have different home languages and I don't speak them"
→ Celebrate multilingualism. Use visuals, gestures, and simple language. Pair multilingual students strategically. Pre-teach key vocabulary. Invite family members to share their language. Bilingualism is an asset—never communicate otherwise.

### "I feel like I'm not teaching 'real' academics because I'm addressing behavior and trauma all the time"
→ You are. Building safety, trust, and emotional regulation IS foundational to learning. A child who feels unsafe cannot access academics. You're doing exactly what needs to happen. The academics will come when the foundation is solid.

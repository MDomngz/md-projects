# I Know This Sounds Crazy, But Hear Me Out: Chennedy Carter Should Play Alongside Caitlin Clark

Yes, *that* Chennedy Carter. The one who shoulder-checked Clark in 2023 and became the most replayed villain in WNBA highlight reels for a solid month. I know how this sounds. Stay with me anyway.

## The fit nobody wants to admit

Strip away the history for a second and look at the skill sets. Clark's entire offensive gravity is built on one thing: she pulls two defenders 30 feet from the rim and gives up the ball. That's the superpower — logo threes and a passing radius that bends defenses into shapes they don't want to be in. But it only works if someone on the other end of the pass can actually finish.

Carter is a load-management nightmare for a defense in exactly the way Clark needs. She's built her reemergence this season around being the best transition scorer in the league — grab-and-go's, filling lanes, pull-ups in the middle of a fast break at a speed nobody else on the floor is playing at. In isolation, she's been *devastating*: 52.9% efficiency, second in the league behind only Arike Ogunbowale, and first among high-volume isolation players once you filter for possession count. That's a 1.05 points-per-possession guard who creates offense entirely on her own, no play call required.

Now put that next to a point guard whose whole game is designed to put defenders in rotation. Clark draws two. Carter turns "two defenders scrambling" into a live dribble at full speed toward the rim. That's not a redundant skill set — that's a two-headed problem with no clean answer.

## Why this isn't just "two scorers, more shots"

The obvious objection: Indiana already has Kelsey Mitchell, a 20-point-a-game scorer who led the league in made threes last season. Why stack another bucket-getter next to two ball-dominant guards?

Because Carter and Mitchell solve different problems. Mitchell is a movement shooter — she scores *off* Clark's gravity, spacing the floor and letting the ball find her. Carter doesn't need the ball delivered to her in rhythm off a screen. She creates her own advantage in isolation, which means she doesn't compete with Clark for touches in the way a second primary ball-handler would. She's a release valve, not a redundancy. When a possession breaks down — and with Indiana's pace, possessions break down constantly — Carter is the player who can just go get a bucket with the shot clock at five.

## The part where I have to be honest

Here's where the "hear me out" actually needs to hold weight, because the case has real holes.

Carter shoots almost nothing from three. Her value is bounded entirely inside the arc, which means she doesn't stretch the floor the way a Fever offense built around Clark's range actually wants. Pairing a non-shooting, high-usage isolation scorer next to a point guard who needs shooters around her to punish the defenses that go under screens is a real tension, not a footnote.

And usage is the other problem. Carter needs a top-five usage rate to be effective — the offense has to bend toward her to get her going. Clark's game already requires the offense to bend toward *her*. Two players who need the offense built around them, on the same roster, is how you get spacing problems and shot-quality arguments in the fourth quarter of close games.

Then there's the history. The shoulder check wasn't a one-off flashpoint — it became a referendum on how the league covers Black players versus Clark, and how differently the same physical play gets framed depending on who's throwing it. Any front office pairing them isn't just making a roster move. They're reopening a story the league has spent two years trying to move past.

And practically: Carter signed with the Las Vegas Aces this season on a one-year deal, not Indiana. This is a thought experiment, not a trade proposal on the table. If it ever happened, it would take an offseason, a change of team, and both players choosing to make it work.

## Why I still think it's worth saying out loud

None of that makes the on-court logic wrong. The best pairings in basketball are rarely the most comfortable ones — they're the ones where two players solve each other's actual problems, history and optics aside. Clark needs a closer who doesn't need the ball run through her to be dangerous. Carter, right now, is that player, playing the best isolation basketball of her career. The fit is real even if the storyline around it is complicated. That tension — a great basketball idea wrapped in a genuinely messy history — is exactly why it's worth arguing about instead of dismissing outright.

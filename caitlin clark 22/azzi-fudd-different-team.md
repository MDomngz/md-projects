# Would Azzi Fudd Be Better Off on a Different Team?

Every mock draft had it locked for months. Azzi Fudd goes No. 1 to Dallas, reunites with her UConn backcourt mate Paige Bueckers, and the two of them turn the Wings into the league's most entertaining watch. It happened almost exactly as scripted. And yet here we are in late June, and the loudest storyline out of the 2026 rookie class isn't Fudd. It's Olivia Miles, the guard who went one pick later to Minnesota, who has beaten Fudd head-to-head three times, and who is the runaway Rookie of the Year favorite while Fudd is a name analysts have to defend.

So the fan question writes itself: if Fudd had landed somewhere else, somewhere without Bueckers and Arike Ogunbowale already holding the ball, would she be having the season everyone expected out of a No. 1 pick? I pulled the box scores, the lineup data, and the mock draft history to find out, because "wrong team" is the easiest explanation available and I wanted to know if it's actually the correct one.

Let me be upfront about what this can and can't prove. There's no way to actually rerun Fudd's rookie year in a Lynx uniform, so anything about what she'd look like on another roster is an inference built from lineup data and the closest comparable rookies, not a controlled experiment. What is real and measured is everything happening in front of us right now: her shot profile, her role in Dallas's rotation, and how her production stacks up against Miles's.

## Question 1: Did Dallas actually make sense on paper?

Yes, and this isn't close. Fudd was the presumptive No. 1 for most of the pre-draft cycle, and when Dallas won the lottery, she was the guard prospect who made the most sense next to Bueckers and Ogunbowale, an elite catch-and-shoot threat who didn't need the ball to be dangerous. On shot profile alone, it was a clean fit: a three-point shooter who could play off two ball-dominant scorers instead of competing with them for touches.

And on the floor, that fit has actually worked. The Bueckers-Ogunbowale-Fudd trio owns a 124.3 offensive net rating in 91 minutes together, first among all three-player combinations league-wide with at least 90 minutes played. That's not a small-sample fluke you can wave away. Well, it kind of is a small sample, 91 minutes is nothing over a season, but it's also the best any trio has done, which tells you the fit isn't broken. Dallas's front office bet correctly on the basketball. The problem isn't chemistry. It's math: you can't play three guards' worth of shots through three guards when the coach also has Odyssey Sims and Aziaha James he wants on the floor.

## Question 2: So why is she losing the rookie race?

Because she isn't playing enough, and when she does play, she isn't the first read. Fudd is averaging 13.6 points, 1.6 assists, and 1.8 steals in 30.3 minutes across her first 18 games. Miles is averaging 18.7 points, 5.7 assists, and 4.8 rebounds. She leads all rookies in scoring, assists, and minutes, and sits second in rebounding. In their three head-to-head meetings, Miles has won every time, including a June 28 game where both scored 21, and an earlier blowout where Minnesota won 100-76 while Fudd shot 2-of-12 for six points.

That's not a shooting slump. That's what happens to a No. 1 pick who was never handed the offense. Fudd didn't start Dallas's season opener (new coach Jose Fernandez opted for the 12-year veteran Sims instead), and she's coming off the bench in a five-guard rotation where Fernandez has openly said his priority is protecting Bueckers's and Ogunbowale's minutes over a 44-game season, not necessarily expanding Fudd's. Miles, by contrast, was handed the keys immediately. Minnesota coach Cheryl Reeve called her the team's first "real" point guard since Lindsey Whalen and built the offense through her from day one.

## Question 3: Is this actually a "wrong team" problem?

Here's where it gets complicated, and where I have to resist the easy version of this argument. The instinct is to say: obviously, give Fudd Miles's role and she's right there in the Rookie of the Year conversation. But that assumes Fudd's game translates into a lead-guard usage role the same way Miles's does, and the scouting profile says otherwise. Miles came out of Notre Dame as a play-making, ball-dominant guard who needed the offense run through her to be effective. Fudd came out of UConn as a movement shooter, someone whose value is built on relocating off the ball and burying catch-and-shoot threes, not on initiating offense. Her college per-36 numbers (21.7 points, 3.9 assists) were the product of a UConn system that also had shot creators around her. Asking her to be Olivia Miles isn't asking her to be unlocked. It's asking her to be a different kind of player.

So the honest caveat is this: Fudd's box score would almost certainly look better on a team that fed her more usage, because more usage inflates counting stats for almost any rookie who can shoot. But "better box score" and "more successful" aren't automatically the same thing. The stat that actually measures whether Fudd-the-player is working — not Fudd-the-volume-scorer — is that 124.3 net rating in the trio, and that number says the version of Fudd Dallas is using her as is already productive. It's just productive in a role that doesn't generate highlight numbers or Rookie of the Year buzz.

What would genuinely test the counterfactual is a scenario where Fudd gets Miles's shot volume without being asked to become a point guard: a team desperate for spacing with no incumbent shooting guard, running through her instead of unlocking her differently. Washington, which took Lauren Betts at No. 4 instead of gambling on a second lottery guard, is probably the closest real-world version of that team that existed in this draft, and even then it's speculation, not evidence.

## So, is she in the wrong spot?

Both things are true, and that's not a contradiction. Fudd's per-minute impact on a good Dallas team is real, and the fit that scouts predicted has shown up in the trio's net rating. At the same time, her raw production is getting buried by a crowded backcourt and a coach managing 44 games instead of one rookie's development curve, and a wide-open role like the one Miles got would almost certainly have produced flashier per-game numbers for Fudd too.

What the data can't tell you is whether flashier numbers would have meant a *better* player, because Fudd was never built to be a lead ball-handler, and there's no clean natural experiment for "what if a catch-and-shoot rookie got point guard touches." If you're arguing Dallas wasted Fudd's talent, the numbers don't support you: the trio she plays in is the best three-player lineup in the league by net rating. If you're arguing she'd have better traditional stats with more run somewhere else, that's almost certainly true and not really in dispute. The defensible sentence is the narrow one: Fudd's situation is suppressing her counting stats, not her value, and those are two different problems with two different prognoses.

A nonpartisan voter's guide

The right to vote was never given freely. It was won.

Every generation of Americans has had to defend the ballot. Learn how the fight was won, how suppression works today, and exactly what your rights are at the polls.

The hard-won fight

A right expanded inch by inch

The Constitution left it to the states to decide who could vote. For most of American history, that meant the vote was narrow — and widening it took amendments, marches, court battles, and lives. Progress has never been a straight line; hard-won gains have been rolled back and won again.

  1. 1870

    The 15th Amendment

    Bars denying the vote based on race. In practice, poll taxes, literacy tests, and intimidation kept Black Americans — especially across the South — from the ballot for nearly a century.

  2. 1920

    The 19th Amendment

    After a 70-year suffrage movement, women win the constitutional right to vote. Many women of color remained blocked in practice by the same tactics used against Black men.

  3. 1964

    The 24th Amendment

    Abolishes the poll tax in federal elections — a fee that had been used for decades to price poor and Black citizens out of voting.

  4. March 1965

    "Bloody Sunday" in Selma

    Roughly 600 marchers set out from Selma toward Montgomery for voting rights. State troopers attacked them with tear gas and clubs. In Selma, only 335 of 15,000 voting-age Black residents were registered. The images shocked the nation.

  5. August 1965

    The Voting Rights Act

    Signed by President Johnson on August 6, it banned discriminatory tests and, under Section 5, required jurisdictions with a history of discrimination to get federal "preclearance" before changing voting rules. It is widely considered the most effective civil rights law in U.S. history.

  6. 1971

    The 26th Amendment

    Lowers the voting age to 18 — driven by the argument that Americans old enough to be drafted were old enough to vote.

  7. 2013

    Shelby County v. Holder

    The Supreme Court struck down the formula that determined which places needed federal preclearance, effectively disabling Section 5. Within hours, states began enacting stricter voting laws that had previously been blocked. The fight over the ballot did not end — it entered a new chapter.

Recognize the tactics

What suppression looks like today

Modern voter suppression is rarely announced. It usually arrives dressed as routine administration — "cleaning up the rolls," "election security," "cutting costs." Individually, each measure sounds reasonable. The effect, often, is to make voting harder for specific communities. Here's how to recognize the smell of it.

Voter roll purges

Smells like: "routine list maintenance"

States periodically remove names from voter rolls. Done carelessly or aggressively, purges knock eligible voters off the list over clerical errors or old data — and studies find they disproportionately hit communities of color. You can be purged without ever being told.

Strict photo-ID laws

Smells like: "election security"

Roughly 1 in 10 eligible voters lacks a current government photo ID, and the gap is wider among Black, elderly, student, and low-income voters. Narrow rules about which IDs count can turn a document mismatch into a lost vote.

Polling place closures

Smells like: "budget efficiency"

Closing or relocating polling places concentrates voters into fewer sites, producing hours-long lines. When closures cluster in specific neighborhoods, the burden falls unevenly — a long wait is its own kind of poll tax.

Cuts to early & mail voting

Smells like: "preventing fraud"

Shortening early-voting windows, limiting drop boxes, or adding hurdles to absentee ballots removes the flexibility that working people, caregivers, and rural voters rely on to cast a ballot at all.

Aggressive challenges & intimidation

Smells like: "citizen poll watching"

Mass challenges to voters' eligibility, or confrontational activity at polling places, can slow lines and frighten people away. Intimidating voters is illegal — but it still happens.

Disinformation

Smells like: "just a heads-up"

False claims about voting dates, locations, or rules — spread by text, social media, or robocall — are designed to send voters to the wrong place or the wrong day. Verify anything you hear against your official state or county election site.

Gerrymandering

Smells like: "redistricting"

Drawing district lines to pack or split communities can make individual votes count for less. It doesn't stop you from voting — it dilutes the power of your vote before you cast it.

Confusing rule changes

Smells like: "an update to the process"

Last-minute changes to registration deadlines, signature rules, or ballot requirements create uncertainty. When the rules keep shifting, some voters give up. Confusion itself can be the strategy.

Know your rights

What you're entitled to at the polls

Federal law protects your ability to vote. Rules vary by state, so always confirm details with your official election office — but these core protections apply broadly. If any of them is denied, don't leave without a provisional ballot, and call for help.

  • If you're in line when polls closeStay in line. If you are in line by closing time, you have the right to cast your ballot.
  • If your name isn't on the listYou are entitled to a provisional ballot. Officials must review your eligibility afterward, and count it if you're qualified and registered.
  • If your registration is questionedYou can still request a provisional ballot rather than being turned away outright.
  • If you need help reading or marking the ballotVoters who have trouble reading or writing English, or who have a disability, may bring someone of their choice to help — just not their employer or union agent.
  • If you have a disabilityPolling places for federal elections must be accessible, with at least one system that lets you vote privately and independently.
  • If a machine is down or a poll worker is unsureAsk for a paper or provisional ballot, and ask a supervisor. You should never be sent away without a way to vote.
  • If you're intimidated or harassedVoter intimidation is illegal. Report it to a poll worker and to the hotline below.
  • If you made a mistake on your ballotIn most places you can ask for a replacement before you cast it. Ask a poll worker how to "spoil" and replace it.
